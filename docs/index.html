Bipedal Robotic Locomotion
with Reinforcement Learning

Drew Burcher · Dan Torri · Nathan Wyatt

Virginia Tech — CS 4824 / ECE 4424 Machine Learning, Spring 2026

📄 Paper (PDF) 📊 Slides (PDF)

Abstract

We train a 13 degree of freedom bipedal humanoid (Booster Robotics T1) to walk forward in a PyBullet simulation using two model-free reinforcement learning algorithms: Soft Actor–Critic (SAC) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The policy is a two-layer MLP that maps joint angle and velocity observations to joint position targets. A reward function was engineered through many trials to combine forward velocity, an alive bonus, an energy cost, and several stability penalties.

After roughly 2.8M environment steps the SAC policy reaches a mean evaluation return of about 7,100 and a forward speed of ~1.6 m/s, while PPO under several reward variants plateaus near a return of 3,000 and never produces a sustained gait. We discuss the reward shaping decisions that drove these results, the catastrophic-forgetting failures we saw with SAC late in training, and the practical trade offs between on and off policy methods on a dense reward locomotion task.

Problem

Getting a bipedal robot to walk is a classic test bed for control and reinforcement learning because the system is high-dimensional, underactuated, and unstable in its natural rest state. Small mistakes early in an episode quickly result in a fall. Classical controllers exist for this problem (zero moment point trackers, model predictive control, quadratic programming whole body controllers), but they all require fairly accurate dynamics models and careful tuning, and the resulting gaits tend to not be robust to varying conditions and environments.

Reinforcement learning gives the model more freedom and flexibility. Instead of hard coding a controller, we specify what we want (move forward, stay upright, save energy) and use gradient descent on a policy for behaviors that maximize that reward. Modern actor–critic methods such as SAC and PPO have made this practical.

Method

Robot & simulator

The Booster Robotics T1 is a ~1.2 m humanoid with 23 revolute joints. We load its URDF in PyBullet and run physics at 240 Hz. The two head joints are locked, and the eight arm joints are held at zero by a stiff position controller, so the policy controls the remaining 13 joints (waist plus six per leg: hip pitch/roll/yaw, knee pitch, and ankle pitch/roll). The policy runs at 60 Hz, so each policy step advances four 240 Hz physics sub steps. Episodes last up to 1,000 policy steps (~16.7 s). An episode terminates early if the torso drops below 0.35 m, rises above 2 m, or tilts past ~70° in roll or pitch.

Observation & action

The observation is a 36-dim float vector containing torso height, torso orientation, torso linear and angular velocity, and the 13 actuated joint positions and velocities. We don't include global x, y position so the policy works the same regardless of position.

The action a ∈ [−1, 1]13 is a normalized joint angle target. We map it to each joint's URDF limits and apply position control with PyBullet's POSITION_CONTROL mode with PD gains Kp=0.2, Kd=0.5. The ankles use more damped gains (Kp=0.1, Kd=1.0) because we found that the robot was moving the ankles very quickly to move forward which was more of a glitch in the physics engine than a viable gait.

Reward

r = 3.0 · v_x                  // forward velocity
  + 2.0                          // alive bonus
  − 0.00495 · mean|τ · q̇|    // energy
  − 0.5 · (roll² + pitch²)    // upright
  − 0.2 · Σ max(|q_i|−0.5, 0)² // joint limits
  − 0.5 · max(−ż, 0)            // anti-drop
  −100   on fall                  // terminal

Algorithms

We use the Stable-Baselines3 implementations of SAC and PPO with a shared [256, 256] MLP for actor and critic, γ=0.99 (discount rate for reward), and Adam at 3×10−4 learning rate. SAC uses a 1 M-transition replay buffer, batch size 256, soft target updates with τ=0.005, and auto-tuned entropy with target entropy −5. PPO uses 2,048 rollout steps, 10 SGD epochs, batch size 64, GAE λ=0.95, clip range 0.2, value coefficient 0.5, and entropy bonus 0.01. Both algorithms run on CPU. For our network sizes and batch sizes this was about 2× faster than GPU in our setup, mostly because the PyBullet step is the bottleneck and that runs on CPU.

Results

SAC: from standing to walking

The policy spends roughly 300 k steps just learning to stand, then transitions into a clear walking phase between 1.0 M and 2.0 M steps, and finally plateaus around an episode return of 6,000 with occasional collapses. By 2.8 M steps the policy walks at ~1.6 m/s and travels ~26 m per 16.7 s episode—a brisk walking pace for a robot of this scale.

Reward composition

Forward velocity grows monotonically as the policy learns to walk, while the survival bonus is by definition constant once the episode runs to the truncation step—together they account for ~82% of the reward at the end of training. The energy penalty is a substantial 12% of the final reward. We tried smaller energy weights and the policy converged to a behavior that "shimmered" the legs at high frequency: it scored well on forward velocity but used roughly twice the torque per meter. The current weight balances those terms reasonably well.

PPO: reward shaping for early gait formation

PPO did not converge to a fully sustained forward gait within the project timeline, but it showed clear behavioral progress across reward iterations. Early PPO policies commonly learned survival-focused strategies: standing upright, remaining mostly stationary, or making only small shuffle attempts. As reward variants were introduced to reduce those stationary solutions, PPO began to expose more directional but still imperfect behaviors, including sideways drift, reverse-motion "crab walk" survival, and forward-fall attempts.

Our strongest PPO result showed the beginning of a true stepping pattern: the robot initiated a visible forward step with knee bend and attempted a follow-up step before losing balance. This was not yet a sustained gait, but it was an improvement over earlier PPO behaviors and demonstrated that reward tuning moved the learned policy in the correct direction. The best PPO checkpoint achieved approximately 1.15 m of forward distance over a 1,000-step evaluation episode, corresponding to an average forward speed of roughly 0.07 m/s.

Conclusion

We trained a 13-DoF bipedal humanoid to walk in PyBullet using two standard deep-RL algorithms and a hand-shaped reward. SAC produced a stable forward gait at ~1.6 m/s after ~2.8 M environment steps; PPO under the same wall-clock budget plateaued at less than half of SAC's return. The biggest practical lesson from the project is how much of the work is in the reward function rather than the algorithm: small changes to the survival bonus or the energy weight produced very different gaits, and most of the time we spent debugging was actually time spent diagnosing reward hacks.

Limitations

  • Catastrophic forgetting. Long SAC runs are not monotonic. After the robot has been walking reliably for hundreds of thousands of steps, it can abruptly lose its balance for a few episodes and collapse the return curve to nearly zero before recovering. We suspect the cause is the replay buffer slowly ageing out the "early-fall" transitions.
  • Unconventional gaits. The robot did not converge to a clean human-style heel-toe gait. The final policy uses what looks more like a duck walk: knees stay bent, ankles push off in a short hop, and the torso leans slightly forward. This is a legitimate local optimum of the reward—it scores well on velocity, survival, and energy. Without a periodic phase reward or a motion-capture imitation term, there is no signal encouraging a particular limb-coordination pattern.
  • Locked arms. Holding the arms at zero simplifies training but also removes a useful degree of freedom for balance. Re-running with the full 21-joint action space is straightforward but would significantly change the credit-assignment problem.
  • Sim-to-real gap. We never deploy on the physical T1, so all numbers are simulation-only. Domain randomization (motor friction, link mass, latency) would be a natural next step before any hardware test.
